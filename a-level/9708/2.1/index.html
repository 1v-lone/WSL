2.1 Demand and Supply Curves


Demand

  • Demand refers to the quantity of a commodity that a consumer is willing and able to buy at a particular price over a given period of time, ceteris paribus.
  • Demand refers to the quantity of a product that purchasers are willing and able to buy at various prices per period of time, all other things being equal
  • Effective demand – the consumer has the ability to pay for the good, i.e the consumer has enough money to buy the good.

Note:

  • Williness to buy:
    Purchasers/Consumers must want a product if they are going to enter into the market with the Intention of buying the good demanded

  • Ability to buy:
    To an economist, the notional demand (speculative and not always backed up by the ability to pay // willing to buy without ability to buy) for a product, which emerges from wanting it, must be backed by purchasing power if the demand is to become an effective demand. Sellers are only willing to sell a product if the purchaser has the money to pay for the product. It is this effective demand that is of particular importance for economists.

Types of Demand

  • Joint or Complementary Demand
    This refers that two goods are demanded together or at the same time. The two goods are called complements. {ie: Cars & Tires}

  • Competitive Demand
    When goods are substitutes for one another, we say that they are in competitive demand. Substitutes are goods that can replace one another or which gives more or less the same satisfaction. They satisfy the same wants. {ie: Coffee & Tea}


Demand Curve

Taking price on y-axis and quantity demanded on x-axis, a downward sloping (negative) curve is known as a demand curve. This demand curve has been drawn as a straight line (a linear relationship). However, tt is perfectly acceptable for price and quantity demanded to be related in a non-linear manner in the form of a type of curve.

Law of demand is only applicable when all other factors are held constant. Assuming ceteris paribus, the law of demand holds these factors constant:
-- Income of consumers.
-- Fashion/taste.
-- Advertising.
-- Population.
-- Prices of substitutes (e.g., Pepsi & Coke).
-- Prices of complements (e.g., car & fuel, pen, & ink, etc...).
-- Future expectations of income and prices of the product.
-- Seasons.
-- Health linkages with the use of product.
-- Income tax.
-- Interest rates (cost of borrowing).
-- Availability of credit.
-- Hire purchase facilities.


Derivation of Market Demand from Individual Demand Curves:

The market demand for a product at a certain price is obtained by summing all of the quantities demanded by individuals at that price. The market demand curve can be obtained through the horizontal summation of all the individual demand curves for the product. Market demand curve Is normally flatter (elastic) than individual demand curves. Changes in Demand:

Demand can change due to:

  • Movement along the same curve (change in quantity demanded).
  • Shift of the demand curve (change in demand).



Factors influencing Demand:

  • Changes in taste and fashion:

    Because of advertising and changes in fashion, demand can change. Consumers may get bored with a particular brand and so switch to another, e.g perfumes, clothing etc,.
    Successful advertising increases awareness and persuades consumers to buy more of a good.

  • Changes in Income:

    The impact of a change in income will depend on the type of good. Normal goods exhibit a positive income effect implying that when income rises, demand will also rise as consumers can now afford more of the good (or raises their purchasing power) while if income falls, demand will fall as consumers can now afford less of the good (or purchasing power falls). On the other hand, an inferior good is one which has a negative income effect and is normally consumed by low income earners and as income increases, the consumer buys less as he/she switch to normal/ superior goods.

  • Changes in population size and structure:

    An increase in the size of the population creates more potential buyers and lead to an increase in the demand for a wide variety of services such as hospitals, schools etc.
    Also, a change in the structure of the population will affect demand for goods and services in the economy. For instance, an increase in the proportion of teenagers in the country would see an increase in the demand for trendy clothes and other accessories such as MP3, PSP etc…..

  • Changes in climatic conditions:

    For instance, during hot season the demand for ice cream and light clothes rises whereas the demand for pullovers falls.

  • Expectation about future price change:

    If people speculate that price will rise in future, they will prefer to buy more now so as not to pay more later, thus increasing demand.

  • Changes in the price of other related goods:

    Goods can be related in terms of substitute or complement.

    A substitute is one that can replace another good. Eg; tea and coffee.
    In the case of substitutes, there is a direct relationship between the price of one good and demand for its substitutes. For instance, if the price of tea rises, then consumers will switch to coffee, thus demand for coffee will increase. And if the price of tea falls, then the demand for coffee will decrease as consumers will switch away from coffee.

    Complements are goods that are demanded together. Eg; car and petrol.

    In the case of complements there is an inverse relationship between price of one good and demand for its complements. For instance, if the price of cars falls, the demand for petrol will rise as people will buy more cars and hence use more petrol.

  • Government policies

    Changes in direct taxes such as income tax may affect demand for goods and services in the economy. For example, an increase in income tax will lead to a fall in the disposable income of consumers and hence lead to a decrease in the demand for many goods and services.


Supply

  • The quantity of a product that producers are willing and able to sell at different prices.
  • Supply refers to the quantities of a product that suppliers are willing and able to sell at various prices per period of time, other things being equal.

Note:

  • Willing and able to sell at various prices:
    In a market economy, companies must gain from selling their products. They are also in the fortunate position that in many cases they can withhold supply if the price is too low. When price rises in the markets, it is assumed companies will be more willing and able to supply more to the market.

The law of supply is only applicable when all other factors are held constant. Assuming ceteris paribus, the law of supply holds these constant:
-- Cost of production
-- Climate\weather (especially for agricultural products).
-- Taxes (e.g., corporation tax, sales tax, VAT, etc.).
-- Subsidies (ie: grants).
-- Changes in Technology.
-- Education and training by the government.
-- Number of producers in the market.
-- Price of jointly supplied goods (e.g., beef and cattle hide).
-- Price of alternatively supplied goods (e.g., leather jackets and leather gloves; brown and black shoes).
-- Techniques of production.

Changes in Supply:




Factors influencing Supply:

  • Changes in climate\weather conditions:

    Change in climate would significantly affect supply of the market, especially for the supply of agricultural products. For instance, bad waether would directly affect the fertility of land for crop plantation resulting in a decrease in supply; Vice-versa

  • Changes in technology:

    Improvement in technology {ie: introduction of machinery} would directly affect and help in archieving lower cost of production and higher productivity which incentivise the producer to supply more inducing supply.

  • Discovery of new supply of raw material / Exhaustion of existing supplies

    Expansion in raw material would significantly affect the supply of an industry as this would allow the firms to induce production scale therefore thrusting the ability to produce; Vise-versa

  • The Entry or Exit of firms into the industry

    Firms operating in the same industry plays an important role in influencing market supply. For instance, if new firms enter the industry, this would directly induce market supply to shift rightwards; Vice-versa

  • Government Policies

    Imposition of tax and subsidies may cause changes in the supply. ie:

    Taxes Subsidies
    The imposition of tax will increase the cost of production for the supplier causing supply to fall. A subsidy is a grant given to production to be incentivized to supply more goods and services in the market at the aim of reduction cost of production and lowering the market price of the good.
